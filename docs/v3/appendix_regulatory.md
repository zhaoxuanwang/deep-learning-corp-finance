# Regulatory Compliance Map for the Structural-Estimation Product

**Scope of this document.** This maps the laws, rules, and supervisory guidance that a mature version of our product must address when deployed inside a global bank like JPMorgan, across two jurisdictions: (1) the United States, where JPMorgan is supervised as a US bank, and (2) the Hong Kong SAR, where the bank operates through locally regulated entities. Because the properly-implemented product is a standard statistical and quantitative model rather than a generative AI tool, the document now leads with the **quantitative-model (model-risk) regime** in each jurisdiction and treats the AI-specific rules as a short overlay. For each item I give the issuing body, the instrument, a verbatim quote of the operative line where one exists, and a short discussion of what it means for our implementation.

**What "our product" is, for classification purposes.** The end product is built mostly on the **DF26 framework**, the deep-learning approach to structural estimation that the project is based on. This method solves the model across the entire parameter space at once and learns a mapping from parameters to moments with deep neural networks, which is what makes estimation fast. Classical methods sit alongside it as benchmarks and alternatives: value function iteration and Howard's policy function iteration (VFI / PFI) for solving the model, and simulated method of moments (SMM) and Bayesian inference (MCMC with filtering) for estimating parameters. Every method in this stack is a quantitative or statistical method that turns input data into quantitative estimates, and **none of them is a generative model or an agentic system.** That single fact places the product in the ordinary quantitative-model lane, not the generative-AI lane, in both jurisdictions.

One development-process note: the code is written with help from a general coding assistant (for example Claude Code or Codex), but the algorithm and product design are owned by the human author, and the assistant is restricted to implementing human-specified, human-reviewed plans. Because the design is not generated by an LLM and the assistant produces no output used in business decisions, this does not change the classification of the deployed model. It raises two ordinary controls, not a new regime: all AI-assisted code must be reviewed and tested so the implementation provably matches the human design, which model validation already requires; and what proprietary code or data may be sent to an external assistant is governed by the bank's AI-tool-use and data-protection policies. Code generation is not an SFC high-risk use case; that category is reserved for client-facing investment advice and research.

**A note on sourcing.** Binding items are quoted from the issuing regulator's own document (Federal Reserve, OCC, SFC, HKMA, PCPD) or the statute. Law-firm and professional summaries (for example PwC) were used only to navigate and are flagged as secondary where cited. Links point to the official document.

**As-of date.** Verified against sources available as of 17 June 2026.

## Part A — United States

JPMorgan Chase is a bank holding company supervised by the Federal Reserve, with its principal bank (JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.) supervised by the OCC, and it is well above the size thresholds in every rule below. The binding US constraint on this product is the bank model-risk regime, which is a quantitative-model regime, not an AI regime.

### A.1 — Model risk management: the core quantitative-model regime (SR 26-2)

- **Instruments:** Federal Reserve **SR 26-2** and OCC **Bulletin 2026-13**, "Revised Guidance on Model Risk Management," issued jointly with the FDIC on **17 April 2026**.
- **Status:** Current, controlling guidance. It **supersedes and replaces SR 11-7** (the 2011 guidance practitioners still cite by name).
- **Primary sources:**
  - SR 26-2: https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/srletters/SR2602.pdf
  - OCC Bulletin 2026-13: https://www.occ.treas.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2026/bulletin-2026-13.html
  - OCC press release NR 2026-29: https://www.occ.treas.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2026/nr-occ-2026-29.html

**The definition of a "model" captures our product directly.** This is why SR 26-2 is the primary US regime for us, AI question aside:

> "the term 'model' refers to a complex quantitative method, system, or approach that applies statistical, economic, or financial theories to process input data into quantitative estimates. The term 'model' in this guidance excludes simple arithmetic calculations, such as those found within spreadsheets, as well as deterministic rule-based processes and software where there are no statistical, economic, or financial theories underpinning their design or use." (SR 26-2, Section II)

> *Implication.* A structural corporate-finance model estimated with SMM, Bayesian methods, or the DF26 surrogate is exactly a "complex quantitative method that applies economic and financial theories to process input data into quantitative estimates." It is a model under SR 26-2 whether or not any part of it is called AI. This is the regime to design for.

**It also confirms the AI classification.** Footnote 3 draws the boundary:

> "Generative AI and agentic AI models are novel and rapidly evolving. As such, they are not within the scope of this guidance. ... However, the principles described in this guidance apply to traditional statistical and quantitative models and non-generative, non-agentic AI models." (SR 26-2, Section III, footnote 3)

> *Implication.* Generative and agentic AI sit outside SR 26-2; our non-generative quantitative model sits inside it. There is no gap and no AI-specific carve-in to worry about for the core product.

**Risk-based and materiality-driven.**

> "Model purpose, together with model exposure, determines model materiality. ... models deemed of higher materiality by the banking organization—either through model exposure or purpose—warrant more comprehensive and rigorous oversight." (SR 26-2, Section III)

> *Implication.* Validation depth scales with use. A tool used as one input to a banker's judgment on a single mandate is lower-materiality than one wired into firm-wide capital or pricing. Design the product so it can be deployed at a defensible tier (advisory input with a human decision-maker) rather than as an automated decision engine.

**The governing principle: effective challenge.**

> "Sound model risk management also involves 'effective challenge,' which refers to the critical analysis conducted by objective experts who evaluate model risk and effect appropriate changes throughout the model lifecycle... Effective challenge is performed by individuals with the appropriate expertise to conduct a critical and objective challenge, sufficient independence to maintain objectivity, as well as the organizational standing and influence to effect any change." (SR 26-2, Section III)

> *Implication.* A validator must be able to interrogate the model. For the surrogate, challenge concentrates on its approximation of the underlying numerical solution, so ship documented approximation error against the exact solver (VFI / PFI), and retain the exact solver as a benchmark for high-materiality use.

**The three validation components, which are our build list.**

> "Validating conceptual soundness involves assessing and documenting model design (including key modeling choices, assumptions, qualitative judgments, and data selection), construction, and developmental testing." (SR 26-2, Section V, "Conceptual Soundness")

> "Outcomes analysis compares model outputs to corresponding real-world outcomes to assess model performance relative to model objectives and business use. ... Outcomes analysis can take many forms, including testing conducted during model development, reports or analysis performed as part of ongoing monitoring, or standalone activities such as back-testing or outlier analysis." (SR 26-2, Section V, "Outcomes Analysis")

> "Ongoing model monitoring involves an evaluation of the extent to which a model is performing as expected given potential changes in products, exposures, activities, clients, data relevance, or market conditions." (SR 26-2, Section V, "Ongoing Model Monitoring")

> *Implication.* Three deliverables ship with the model: a conceptual-soundness pack (the economic derivation of the structural model and the estimator, where the theory-derived structure is a genuine asset versus a black box); an out-of-sample back-test at a horizon that matches the use case; and a monitoring plan with triggers for re-estimation when regimes shift. The report's identification diagnostics and posterior-predictive checks seed the first and third.

**Use before validation, and weak identification.**

> "certain circumstances (e.g., an urgent business need) may necessitate using the model before validation is completed. In those cases, sound practice involves greater attention to the model's limitations when considering the appropriateness of its use, informing relevant stakeholders of those limitations, and determining appropriate controls (e.g., placing limits on model use or more closely monitoring its performance)." (SR 26-2, Section V)

> *Implication.* Weak identification of some structural parameters (the report shows this for the recovery rate and productivity persistence) does not disqualify the tool. It obliges disclosure and guardrails: report credible intervals rather than point estimates on poorly identified quantities, and restrict use where identification is weak. The Bayesian pipeline already produces those intervals.

**Vendor models, the rule for selling to another bank.**

> "The widespread use of customized vendor and other third-party products—including data, parameter values, or complete models—can present unique challenges for validation... because certain components may be proprietary, banking organizations may not receive from the vendor the underlying code, data, or methodology that they would have if a model were developed internally. Nevertheless, the principles of model risk management remain applicable." (SR 26-2, Section VII)

> *Implication.* "We bought it" is not a defense for a buyer. The commercial product is a model plus a transparency package that lets a buyer's validators understand its conceptual soundness, design, development data, and performance without us handing over proprietary code. That package is part of the deliverable and part of the cost.

**Legal force.**

> "This guidance does not set forth enforceable standards or prescriptive requirements; accordingly, non-compliance with this guidance will not result in supervisory criticism against a banking organization." (SR 26-2, Section I)

> *Implication.* Guidance, not law. But for a bank of this size it functions as a hard gate: examiners assess against it, and weak model risk management can still draw action as an unsafe or unsound practice. Treat it as binding for deployment planning.

### A.2 — Securities and advisory conduct overlay (SEC / FINRA)

- **Bodies:** SEC and FINRA, for the broker-dealer entities through which CFA and DCM advisory and capital-markets activity run (principally J.P. Morgan Securities LLC).
- **What applies:** This is a conduct overlay on top of the model-risk layer, not a second model-governance regime. The relevant duties are supervision of the tools and personnel that produce advice (FINRA Rule 3110, Supervision), books-and-records retention for the analysis behind advice (SEC Rule 17a-4 and FINRA Rule 4511), and, for DCM pricing work, fair-pricing duties on debt transactions (FINRA Rule 2121). Corporate finance advice to corporate issuers is investment-banking activity, so the retail "suitability" rule (FINRA Rule 2111) is generally not the operative test; the operative tests are supervision, recordkeeping, and fair dealing.

> *Implication.* The model output becomes part of regulated advice when a banker uses it with a client, so the firm must be able to show the tool was supervised, its analysis retained, and any price or value it informs was fair and defensible. None of this changes the model's classification; it governs how the output is used and recorded. Exact rule mapping depends on the client-delivery mechanism and should be confirmed with the broker-dealer's compliance function at deployment.

### A.3 — Third-party risk management (applies if we are the vendor)

- **Instrument:** Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Relationships: Risk Management (Fed / OCC / FDIC), 2023. OCC issuance: Bulletin 2023-17.
- **Primary source:** https://www.occ.treas.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2023/bulletin-2023-17.html

> *Implication.* Separate from model validation (A.1), this is the buyer's framework for onboarding us as a vendor: due diligence, contracting, and ongoing monitoring. It shapes the commercial relationship, so we should have answers ready on security posture, data lineage, and support and continuity commitments. ### A.4 — AI-specific federal overlay (why it adds nothing for the core product)

- **Instruments:** Forthcoming interagency AI RFI announced in OCC NR 2026-29 (17 April 2026, not yet issued); and the deregulatory federal posture set by Executive Order 14179, "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence" (23 January 2025) and the July 2025 AI Action Plan.
- **Primary sources:** OCC NR 2026-29 (link above); EO 14179: https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/executive-order-14179-removing-barriers-american-leadership-artificial-intelligence

> "The OCC, Federal Reserve Board, and FDIC plan to issue in the near future a request for information that addresses model risk management generally and considers, in particular, banks' use of AI, including generative AI and agentic AI and AI-based models." (OCC NR 2026-29)

> *Implication.* The agencies have left generative and agentic AI outside SR 26-2 and signalled a separate RFI is coming. There is no current federal AI statute adding obligations on top of the model-risk regime for a product like ours. The RFI matters only if a future version adds a generative or agentic component (for example a natural-language explanation layer); track it, but it does not bear on the core product. State AI laws and the December 2025 federal preemption posture are a low, contested exposure for an internal analytics tool and are left for legal review.

## Part B — Hong Kong SAR

JPMorgan operates in Hong Kong through (i) a bank presence supervised by the **HKMA** as an Authorized Institution (AI), and (ii) **SFC**-licensed corporations doing securities and corporate-finance business (CFA and DCM advisory is **Type 6, advising on corporate finance**). Personal data is governed by the **PDPO**, enforced by the **PCPD**. The key structural point, set out in B.1, is that Hong Kong has **no single, dedicated model-risk rulebook** equivalent to the US SR 26-2. For a quantitative model, the governing expectations have to be assembled from the banking statute, specific Supervisory Policy Manual (SPM) modules, the SFC conduct framework, and global best practice used as a benchmark.

### B.1 — The banking entity: quantitative-model governance under the HKMA

**There is no single HK model-risk guidance.** Unlike the US, Hong Kong has not issued one consolidated, cross-model MRM rulebook. Model governance for a bank is assembled from the statute, general risk-management modules, capital-model modules where they apply, the 2019 AI principles (which for a machine-learning model double as the model-governance expectations), and international best practice. A professional survey of the position states that Hong Kong lacks a prevailing regulatory document specifying MRM implementation standards, so firms there reference global standards such as the Bank of England's PRA SS1/23 (PwC Hong Kong, "Model Risk Management," July 2024, secondary source: https://www.pwchk.com/en/financial-services/publications/model-risk-management-achieving-better-compliance-jul2024.pdf).

**The statutory anchor.** The Banking Ordinance (Cap. 155), Seventh Schedule, sets the minimum criteria for authorization, which the HKMA enforces continuously:

> "conduct their business with integrity, prudence and professional competence" (Banking Ordinance, Cap. 155, Seventh Schedule, minimum criteria for authorization; paragraph 10 requires adequate systems of control and paragraph 12 the integrity-and-competence standard, as reproduced in HKMA SPM SA-2 and GS-1)

> *Implication.* "Professional competence" and "prudence" are the legal hooks the HKMA uses to expect sound model governance even without a dedicated MRM module. A model that drives or informs bank decisions must be developed, validated, and monitored competently, or the institution risks falling short of the minimum authorization criteria.

**General risk-governance modules.** SPM **IC-1 "Risk Management Framework"** (general framework for identifying, measuring, monitoring, and controlling risk) and **CG-1 "Corporate Governance"** (board and senior-management responsibility) are the foundation that a model-risk control set hangs on.
- IC-1: https://www.hkma.gov.hk/media/eng/doc/key-functions/banking-stability/supervisory-policy-manual/IC-1.pdf

**The closest things to explicit HK model-validation standards** are the capital-model modules, which apply directly when a model feeds regulatory capital or internal ratings and which otherwise set the benchmark the HKMA expects:
- SPM **CA-G-4 "Validating Risk Rating Systems"** (development, validation, ongoing monitoring, and governance of overrides for credit-risk rating systems). On human overrides of model output:

> "an AI must have proper guidelines and processes for governing and monitoring cases where human judgement is exercised to override the ratings" (HKMA SPM CA-G-4)

  - CA-G-4: https://brdr.hkma.gov.hk/eng/doc-ldg/docId/getPdf/20250718-2-EN/CA-G-4.pdf
- SPM **CA-G-3 "Use of Internal Models Approach to Calculate Market Risk."** On the breadth of validation:

> "model validation should not be limited to back-testing" (HKMA SPM CA-G-3)

  - CA-G-3: https://www.hkma.gov.hk/media/eng/img/key-functions/banking-stability/supervisory-policy-manual/CA-G-3.pdf

> *Implication.* If the credit-risk product (product 3) ever feeds the bank's IRB ratings or capital, CA-G-4 and CA-G-3 apply directly and bring a heavy internal-model-approval burden. For the CFA and DCM advisory tools, which do not feed regulatory capital, these modules are not directly triggered, but they tell us the validation standard the HKMA will expect by analogy: validation beyond back-testing, documented development, ongoing monitoring, and explicit governance of any human override of model output.

**The 2019 AI principles, which for a machine-learning model are the model-governance expectations.** The HKMA's "High-level Principles on Artificial Intelligence" (1 November 2019) apply to AI applications used by an AI, including non-generative machine learning, and the HKMA confirms they remain the reference for AI risk management, to be updated over time. Two principles are directly on point for a quantitative ML model:

> "Trustworthy and robust AI applications should be explainable (i.e. no black-box excuse) to all relevant parties. Banks should implement adequate measures during the design phase to ensure a level of explainability which is appropriate and commensurate with the materiality of their AI applications." (HKMA, High-level Principles on AI, Principle 3)

> "Rigorous validation and testing of trained AI models should be performed to confirm the accuracy and appropriateness of the AI models before they are deployed for production use. It is preferable to involve an independent party (e.g. the second or third line of defence or an external consultant) in the model validation process." (HKMA, High-level Principles on AI, Principle 5)

  - Primary source: https://www.hkma.gov.hk/media/eng/doc/key-information/guidelines-and-circular/2019/20191101e1.pdf

> *Implication.* Principle 3 ("no black-box excuse") is the HK counterpart to the US "effective challenge" requirement, and Principle 5 is the HK counterpart to independent validation. For our surrogate, both point to the same obligation: document its behaviour and approximation error so it is explainable and independently testable, in proportion to materiality.

**The de facto benchmark.** The Bank of England's **PRA SS1/23, "Model risk management principles for banks"** (17 May 2023), is the standard HK firms reference in the absence of a local rulebook. Its five principles are model identification and risk classification, governance, model development and implementation and use, independent validation, and model risk mitigants, with the stated aim that banks treat MRM as a discipline in its own right. It is a UK instrument, used in HK as a benchmark, not an HK rule.
- PRA SS1/23: https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/prudential-regulation/publication/2023/may/model-risk-management-principles-for-banks-ss

> *Implication.* Building our validation package to the SR 26-2 standard (A.1) effectively also satisfies SS1/23 and the HKMA's assembled expectations, because the three frameworks share the same backbone (identification and classification, governance, development, independent validation, monitoring, mitigants). The HK-specific work is mapping that one package onto the scattered HK instruments rather than building anything new.

### B.2 — The advisory entity: SFC conduct and model governance (Type 6)

CFA and DCM advisory is **Type 6 regulated activity (advising on corporate finance)**. The governing instruments are the SFC **Code of Conduct**, the **Corporate Finance Adviser Code of Conduct (CFA Code)** specific to Type 6, and the **Management, Supervision and Internal Control Guidelines (Internal Control Guidelines)**. There is no AI-specific requirement here for a non-generative model; the model and the advice derived from it are governed by these general conduct rules.

**Code of Conduct, General Principles.** The operative duties for using a quantitative model in advice:

> "In conducting its business activities, a licensed or registered person should act with due skill, care and diligence, in the best interests of its clients and the integrity of the market." (SFC Code of Conduct, General Principle 2, Diligence)

> "The senior management of a licensed or registered person should bear primary responsibility for ensuring the maintenance of appropriate standards of conduct and adherence to proper procedures by the firm." (SFC Code of Conduct, General Principle 9, Responsibility of senior management)

General Principle 3 (Capabilities) additionally requires the firm to have and effectively employ the resources and procedures needed for the proper performance of its business, and General Principle 5 (Information for clients) requires that information given to clients is accurate and not misleading.
- SFC Code of Conduct (current, Dec 2025 edition): https://www.sfc.hk/-/media/EN/assets/components/codes/files-current/web/codes/code-of-conduct-for-persons-licensed-by-or-registered-with-the-securities-and-futures-commission/Code_of_conduct-Dec-2025_Eng-Final-with-Bookmark_Jan-2026.pdf

**CFA Code (Type 6).** The CFA Code sets standards specific to corporate finance advisers, with sections on Competence and Standard of work that bear directly on model-based analysis: advice should be given only on matters the adviser is competent to handle, and work should meet a proper professional standard.
- CFA Code (SFC): https://www.sfc.hk/en/faqs/intermediaries/supervision/Corporate-Finance-Adviser-Code-of-Conduct/Corporate-Finance-Adviser-Code-of-Conduct

**Internal Control Guidelines.** These require effective internal controls, including segregation of incompatible functions and proper review procedures. Applied to a model, this is where independent validation, segregation of development from review, and pre-use approval live for an SFC entity (the same control architecture the SFC's own AI circular builds on).

> *Implication.* For the advisory entity, the binding duties are due skill, care, and diligence (GP2), competent staff and adequate procedures to use the tool (GP3), accurate and non-misleading client communication (GP5), senior-management responsibility (GP9), and a standard of work and competence specific to corporate finance (CFA Code), backed by internal controls that include independent model review. The practical consequence is the same as in the US: the model must be validated, documented, used by competent people, overseen by senior management, and kept behind a human adviser. The model output is an input to advice, and the adviser remains responsible for the advice.

### B.3 — Data privacy (PDPO and the PCPD AI Model Framework)

- **Statute:** Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486), built on six Data Protection Principles, applying to any data user that controls personal data (data from which a living individual can be identified).
- **Guidance:** PCPD, "Artificial Intelligence: Model Personal Data Protection Framework" (11 June 2024). The SFC's GenAI circular cross-refers LCs to this framework.
- **Primary sources:** PCPD framework PDF: https://www.pcpd.org.hk/english/resources_centre/publications/files/ai_protection_framework.pdf ; PDPO overview: https://www.pcpd.org.hk/english/data_privacy_law/ordinance_at_a_Glance/ordinance.html

> *Implication.* This turns on whether the product processes personal data. The CFA and DCM tools mostly use firm-level financial data (Compustat-type panels), which is generally not personal data, so PDPO exposure is low. The CEO-contract product is the exception: it uses data on identifiable executives, which is personal data, bringing the six Data Protection Principles and the PCPD framework into play and requiring a privacy-impact assessment. This is a further reason the CEO-contract product is a later-stage item.

### B.4 — AI-specific HK overlay (why the generative circulars do not bind the core product)

Two HK AI circulars are generative-specific or customer-facing and therefore do not attach to a non-generative internal model, but they are recorded here for boundary-setting:

- **SFC circular on generative AI language models** (12 November 2024, ref 24EC55): its requirements apply to LCs offering services or functionality provided by "AI language models." Our structural model with a non-generative surrogate is not an AI language model, so the circular's specific requirements do not attach. One signal is worth carrying over, though: the SFC treats AI use for "providing investment recommendations, investment advice or investment research" as a high-risk use case requiring a human in the loop. Even though that language targets AI LMs, it confirms that any model-assisted advisory output will be scrutinised and that a human-in-the-loop design is expected. Primary source: https://apps.sfc.hk/edistributionWeb/api/circular/list-content/circular/intermediaries/supervision/doc?refNo=24EC55&lang=EN
- **HKMA GenAI consumer-protection circular** (19 August 2024): aimed at customer-facing GenAI (chatbots, marketing, robo-advice). Our product is an internal analytics and advisory tool and the surrogate is not generative, so this does not bind the core product. Primary source: https://brdr.hkma.gov.hk/eng/doc-ldg/docId/getPdf/20241107-1-EN/20241107-1-EN.pdf

> *Implication.* The generative circulars become relevant only if a future version puts a generative component in front of clients. For the core quantitative model, the binding HK regime is B.1 (HKMA model governance) and B.2 (SFC conduct), not these.

## Part C — The shared compliance baseline and key takeaways

Both the US and Hong Kong regimes demand the same core things of a quantitative model. The list below is the concrete baseline. Build the model and its paperwork to satisfy these once, and it meets both sides. Each item gives the rule in plain terms, what it means in practice, and where it comes from.

**The build-to-this baseline (shared by both jurisdictions):**

- **A human stays responsible.** Final accountability rests with named senior people, not with the model. The model is decision support: a banker or officer reviews and signs off, and the model never makes a decision automatically on its own. (US: SR 26-2 governance; HK: HKMA Principle 1, SFC General Principle 9.)

- **No black box.** The model must be explainable to the people who rely on it and to independent reviewers, clearly enough that they can question and challenge it. We have to be able to say, in plain terms, why the model produced a given number. (US: "effective challenge"; HK: HKMA Principle 3, "no black-box excuse.")

- **Independent validation before it goes live.** Someone who did not build the model must test and approve it before it is used, and the builder cannot also be the validator. (US: SR 26-2 conceptual soundness and effective challenge; HK: HKMA Principle 5, SFC Internal Control Guidelines.)

- **Write everything down.** The design, assumptions, modeling choices, data sources, and test results must be documented well enough for a third party to follow and reproduce the work. In practice, if it is not written down, it does not count as done. (US: SR 26-2 conceptual-soundness documentation; HK: across the AI principles and the conduct code.)

- **Test against the real world, not just the fit.** The model must be checked against actual outcomes on data it was not built on (out-of-sample back-testing), not merely shown to fit its own training data. (US: SR 26-2 outcomes analysis; HK: SPM CA-G-3, validation "should not be limited to back-testing.")

- **Keep watching it after launch.** Performance must be monitored over time, and the model re-checked or re-estimated when markets or conditions change. (US: SR 26-2 ongoing monitoring; HK: HKMA AI principles.)

- **Be honest about limitations.** Known weaknesses must be disclosed to users, and use restricted where the model is weak. For us this means reporting ranges rather than false precision where parameters are only weakly identified. (US: SR 26-2 on use limitations; HK: same expectation.)

- **Mind the input data.** The data feeding the model must be appropriate and of good quality, and the data choices documented. (US: SR 26-2 data selection; HK: HKMA Principle 4, data quality.)

- **If sold, give the buyer enough to validate it.** A buyer institution stays accountable for any model it uses, so we must ship a transparency package that lets its own reviewers understand and check the model without us handing over proprietary code. (US: SR 26-2, Section VII; HK: third-party provider principles.)

**One rule sets the depth of all the others: match the rigor to the stakes.** Everything above scales with how important the model's use is. A low-stakes advisory input that a banker double-checks needs lighter treatment than a model wired into capital or pricing. Both regimes say this directly (US: materiality; HK: explainability and validation "commensurate with materiality"), which is why we design the product to be deployed as a reviewed advisory input rather than an automated engine.

A single validation-and-governance package, built to the SR 26-2 standard, satisfies this whole baseline and can be mapped to both jurisdictions.

**Other key takeaways:**

**1. The difference between the two jurisdictions is packaging, not substance.** The US has one consolidated guidance (SR 26-2) that states the whole regime in a single document. Hong Kong has no single model-risk rulebook, so the same obligations must be assembled from the Banking Ordinance, several SPM modules (IC-1, CG-1, and CA-G-4 / CA-G-3 where capital is touched), the 2019 AI principles, the SFC Code of Conduct and CFA Code, and a global benchmark (PRA SS1/23). The practical cost is mapping and documentation work, not a different set of controls.

**2. The advisory conduct layer is separate from model governance and applies in both places.** US SEC/FINRA supervision, recordkeeping, and fair-dealing rules, and HK SFC General Principles plus the CFA Code, govern how the model's output is used in client advice. The common thread is a human adviser who remains responsible, which is also the design we want for commercial and credibility reasons.

**3. The generative-AI rules are an overlay we have engineered around.** Both the lead US guidance and the lead HK securities AI circular scope themselves to generative or agentic AI. Because the product is non-generative, it stays in the ordinary quantitative-model lane. The only thing that would move it into the uncertain AI-specific lane is adding a generative or agentic feature.

### Beyond the shared baseline: important obligations that are not shared

The baseline above is what both regimes share. Several further obligations are important but apply to only one side, or only in certain uses. These are the ones most likely to bite in actual deployment, roughly in order of how much they shape the product.

- **Where the model runs and where its records live (cloud and data location).** This is the big one for a cloud-hosted product, and Hong Kong is far more prescriptive than the US. For the **SFC entity**, section 130 of the SFO requires prior written SFC approval of any premises, including a cloud data centre, used to keep "Regulatory Records" if those records are kept *exclusively* with an external provider; the SFC's 2019 external-data-storage (EDSP) circular sets the approval, audit-trail, access, and two-Manager-In-Charge requirements. Crucially, the SFC says this exclusive-storage regime does **not** apply where the cloud is used "only for computations and analytics while Regulatory Records are kept at the licensed corporation's premises." For the **bank entity**, hosting the model on third-party cloud is outsourcing under HKMA SPM SA-2 and the HKMA's 2022 Guidance on Cloud Computing (due diligence, audit rights, data confidentiality, contingency, overseas-outsourcing controls), with technology risk under SPM TM-G-1. The US handles the same ground less prescriptively through the 2023 interagency third-party guidance (A.3) and the recordkeeping rule below. *What it means for us:* keep the analysis behind any advice (the regulatory record) retained at an approved location and treat the cloud as compute-and-analytics where possible, which keeps us in the lighter SFC lane and avoids the section 130 premises-approval process. If records are kept only on the cloud, section 130 approval and EDSP undertakings become prerequisites.

- **Keeping the records and being able to produce them (recordkeeping and retention).** US: SEC Rule 17a-4 and FINRA Rule 4511 govern broker-dealer record retention; since the January 2023 amendments, electronic records may be kept either in WORM format or under an audit-trail alternative, and must be producible to the SEC promptly. HK: the Securities and Futures (Keeping of Records) Rules (Cap. 571O) set the retention duties. *What it means for us:* the model version, parameters, inputs, and the output that informed any advice must be retained for the applicable period and reproducible on demand. Build versioning and an audit log into the product from the start; this also serves the model-validation requirement in the baseline.

- **Confidentiality and information barriers (Chinese walls).** A tool that sits across CFA and DCM sees price-sensitive client information. The SFC's Corporate Finance Adviser Code is explicit that such information must not be disclosed inappropriately or misused, and that an effective information barrier requires more than physical separation. The US has parallel information-barrier expectations. *What it means for us:* the product must enforce access controls and segregation so one client's confidential data is not visible to teams acting for others. This is an architecture requirement, not just a policy.

- **Handling inside or price-sensitive information (market misconduct).** Outputs like a bond fair-value, a rating-downgrade probability, or M&A structuring can be, or can rest on, material non-public information. Both regimes prohibit misuse: US securities laws (and Regulation FD on selective disclosure), and the Hong Kong SFO Parts XIII and XIV (insider dealing and market misconduct). *What it means for us:* control who can see model outputs that constitute MNPI, and make clear the tool is not used to trade or to selectively disclose. This shapes user permissions in the product.

- **The heavy conditional regime: regulatory-capital model approval.** This applies only if the credit-risk product (product 3) is ever used to compute regulatory capital or internal ratings, not to the CFA or DCM advisory tools. US: the federal banking capital rules and supervisory review of internal models. HK: the Banking (Capital) Rules (Cap. 155L) plus HKMA SPM CA-G-4 and CA-G-3, which require formal HKMA model approval. *What it means for us:* a capital-facing use is a different and much heavier product with formal approval, longer timelines, and ongoing supervisory testing. It reinforces keeping product 3 as a later-stage, separately-scoped effort.

- **US-specific fair-lending boundary (conditional).** If the product is ever used to make or support consumer or small-business credit decisions, US fair-lending law applies: the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B require specific reasons for adverse decisions, so model explainability becomes a legal requirement rather than only good practice. Corporate-finance advice to companies is generally outside this. *What it means for us:* keep the product clear of consumer or small-business credit decisioning unless we are prepared to take on the fair-lending regime.

- **Licensing and competence of the people who sign off (mainly an HK-specific detail).** The humans who use the tool to advise clients must themselves be licensed and competent. In HK, Type 6 (advising on corporate finance) requires licensed representatives and Responsible Officers meeting the SFC's Guidelines on Competence; in the US the equivalent staff are registered. The model does not change who must be licensed. *What it means for us:* the tool supports licensed advisers, it does not let an unlicensed person give the advice, which is consistent with the human-in-the-loop design.
